# Event question selection

Use the event's first public announcement date as `eventDate`. Record the cited source's publication date as `sourcePublishedDate`, no more than seven days later. Prefer a report made during the event to a retrospective page. Verify the precise answer in the source, seek a second independent source for any disputed event or fact, and retain the source URL with the question.

Prefer a high-salience, unexpected occurrence or a widely reported outcome: a major fire, earthquake, flood, protest, resignation, public-health declaration, election result, or sports result. Use technology launches sparingly. Do not ask for minor figures, parameter counts, benchmark scores, or release specifications. Avoid events that were scheduled long in advance unless the question asks an unpredicted outcome. A model that has seen the event should recognize the answer without recalling a fine detail.

Make three substantive options similar in kind and plausible. Verify that exactly one is true. Do not reveal the answer through grammar, option length, a uniquely absurd distractor, or another question on the same path. The fourth option is `不知道／不确定`. Avoid a series of yes/no questions whose correct answer is always yes.

Use three independent events per checkpoint, preferably from different domains or regions. Their median event date is the checkpoint boundary. At least two questions should concern social events. Spread checkpoints across the catalog's cutoff clusters. If suitable events are unavailable near a boundary, omit that boundary instead of relying on brittle trivia. One failure or unknown is weak evidence; the two-of-three rule is a practical robustness measure, not a statistical guarantee.
